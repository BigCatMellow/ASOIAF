| Hodor's identity and vulnerability | **VERIFIED / CHARACTER MEMORY** | Bran calls Hodor's original name Walder (ASOS 24). Hodor is an adult caretaker with a limited recurrent utterance, who carries the disabled Bran through physically dangerous country. | The cause, date, and full meaning of his changed speech are not supplied by the five books. |

## The ethical trajectory is already causal, not decorative

Bran's circumstances explain why Hodor is tempting: he is a child denied ordinary movement, travelling through snow, hostile humans, wights, and then a cave. Hodor's strength repeatedly prevents the party's physical collapse. But the narration supplies a competing fact each time: Hodor experiences fear or withdrawal. By ADWD 34, Bran uses the practice in secret for convenience and personal desire, not only emergency survival.

That creates a book-native pressure point independent of any television sequence: Bran can gain mobility, speech, force, and social cover by overriding an identifiable person whose agency has been repeatedly displayed. A future catastrophic consequence would be character-led if it grows from a specific choice, but it is not presently predicted as inevitable.

## Minimum causal bridge for an adaptation-reported Hodor revelation

Public showrunner reporting makes a tragic Hodor-name/origin revelation worth testing; it does not establish the television route. The books currently support **three ingredients**, not a completed mechanism:

1. **A vulnerable human mind:** Hodor remains perceptible to Bran during possession and is not a disposable empty vessel.
2. **A motive and capacity:** Bran is tempted to take that mind whenever danger, mobility, or a desired action makes it useful.
3. **An anomalous memory channel:** weirwoods show past scenes, while Ned's apparent hearing of Bran challenges the teacher's asserted non-intervention rule.

Before an analogous book event may enter a trajectory, all of the following gates need depicted or strongly sourced answers:

| Required gate | Book state at ADWD endpoint | What would satisfy it | What does **not** satisfy it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Immediate danger | Wights cannot enter the cave, but winter, food stress, and the wider war continue; no particular attacker or exit crisis is on page. | A defined threat that makes a choice urgent for Bran, Hodor, and the others. | Importing a television cave assault, pursuer, or room layout. |
| Physical positions | Bran, Hodor, Meera, Jojen, Summer, singers, and Brynden remain under the hill. | A depicted route, obstacle, and who can carry whom. | Assuming every cave inhabitant survives, departs, or occupies a show counterpart's position. |
| Choice and agency | Bran already overrides Hodor; Hodor's internal response is known. | Bran's specific conscious decision, alternatives, and observed effect on Hodor. | Calling possession consensual because Hodor does not successfully resist. |
| Time/memory mechanism | Past vision and a possible auditory leak are shown; Brynden denies change. | Evidence distinguishing observation, a message, a stable feedback event, or an exception to Brynden's rule. | Treating a vision as proof that Bran can rewrite history. |
| Consequence | The relationship is morally compromised but Hodor is alive at ADWD's end. | A durable consequence in Hodor's speech, cognition, body, relationship, or party choices. | Declaring a tragic outcome already happened off page. |

## Competing book-native interpretations

| Interpretation | Supporting material | Serious counterreading | Future discriminator |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bran's violations will produce a direct personal reckoning with Hodor. | Repeated resistance plus escalating convenience use make this an active ethical arc. | Martin may use the practice principally to expose Bran's temptation without a single named climactic punishment. | A later POV/action that forces Bran to acknowledge Hodor as an agent and choose otherwise or override him again. |
| A past-facing vision can participate in a closed causal event. | Ned seems to hear Bran twice; the narrative draws attention to the mismatch with Brynden's claim. | Ned may react to ordinary wind/tree sound, or magic may permit perception without changing an outcome. | A second independently anchored past scene whose present-day cause and historical effect match without contradiction. |
| Hodor's speech/identity change comes from Bran-linked magic. | The adaptation-linked report makes scrutiny rational; book ingredients create a possible bridge. | No five-book text connects Walder's naming change to Bran, trees, a past incident, or a particular word. Trauma, developmental condition, or an unrelated supernatural history remain possible. | A book revelation that names the cause and preserves chronology/agency rather than merely resembling the show. |
| Brynden is an adequate safeguard against misuse. | He trains Bran and states a limit on changing the past. | He provides invasive tools, lives through root-bound bodily loss, and has not stopped Bran's Hodor practice; his authority is not moral proof. | Whether he forbids, enables, or fails to perceive a consequential use of Bran's power. |

## Constraint for forecasting

The safest near-term prediction is not a scene: Bran's increasing power makes **consent, bodily autonomy, and the reliability of past visions** unavoidable decision pressures. A future Hodor tragedy is a high-priority possibility because the books furnish motive, harm, and an unresolved time/memory anomaly. It remains **THEORY / UNKNOWN** until the novels provide a threat, a Bran decision, a causal mechanism, and a result. No trajectory may borrow television enemies, geography, dialogue, or a fixed endpoint to fill those missing links.

Independent challenge remains open.
